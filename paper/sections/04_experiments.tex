\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets, metrics, and splits}

We evaluate on six standard change-detection benchmarks:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{LEVIR-CD}~\citep{chen2020spatial} --- building change,
    7120/1024/2048 train/val/test 256$^2$ crops of the standard split.
  \item \textbf{LEVIR-CD+}~\citep{zheng2022changestar} --- extended LEVIR,
    637/348 train/test 1024$^2$ tiles; we hold out 10\% of train as val.
  \item \textbf{S2Looking}~\citep{shen2021s2looking} --- 3500/500/1000
    rural, off-nadir 1024$^2$ tiles.
  \item \textbf{CDD}~\citep{lebedev2018change} --- 10000/2998/3000 256$^2$
    crops with seasonal variation.
  \item \textbf{DSIFN-CD}~\citep{zhang2020deeply} --- we use the
    10000/2998/3000 split from the official \texttt{Real/subset}; we name
    the split explicitly because cross-paper comparisons can differ by
    $\sim$5 F1.
  \item \textbf{SECOND}~\citep{yang2021second} (binary collapse) ---
    2968/1694 train/test; val is a 10\% auto-split. Binary change masks are
    generated as $M = \mathbf{1}[\ell^{(r)} \ne \ell^{(t)}]$ over labelled
    pixels.
\end{itemize}

We report F1, IoU, Precision, Recall, Overall Accuracy (OA), and Cohen's
$\kappa$ at threshold 0.5 (or the val-selected threshold when noted). On
CDD, F1 saturates above 95 in recent literature; we report IoU as the
primary discriminator following~\citep{corley2024reality}.

\paragraph{Fair comparison.} For every benchmark, our augmentation
pipeline \emph{matches the published SOTA method's pipeline verbatim}. We
release these as YAML configs in \texttt{configs/datasets/<name>.yaml}.
Augmentations not used by any top-3 method on a benchmark (e.g.\ Mixup,
CutMix, elastic distortion, random grayscale) are explicitly excluded.
Inference is single-pass with optional 8-way TTA; threshold can be tuned
on val (see \S\ref{sec:cheap-wins}). No ensembles, no model averaging.

\paragraph{Comparison policy --- methods using extra change-detection data.}
We restrict our SOTA comparison to methods trained \emph{only} on each
benchmark's released training set (with the standard caveat that all
methods, including ours, use foundation-model pretraining on general-image
corpora such as ImageNet, DINOv2, CLIP, or SAM). We \emph{exclude} from the
SOTA bar:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The entire \textbf{ChangeStar family}~\citep{zheng2022changestar,
    zheng2023changen, zheng2024changestar2}, including ChangeStar (2021),
    ChangeStar+Changen (2023, $+90$\,k synthetic bitemporal pairs from
    a learned generator), and ChangeStar2 (2024, Changen2 pretraining).
    These methods belong to the same architectural family and the
    Changen-pretrained variants depend on synthetic CD data we do not
    generate; we report their numbers in italics as reference rows
    only.
  \item \textbf{UniChange}~\citep{zeng2025unichange} jointly trains on
    LEVIR-CD, S2Looking, SECOND, and WHU-CD --- importing labelled CD
    examples from other benchmarks into each evaluation. Excluded.
\end{itemize}
This is the only fair scope for our claims, since GALASH never sees CD
labels outside the benchmark it is evaluated on. Methods that use only
foundation-model pretraining (DDPM-CD's RS-image SSL; SChanger's IAILD
segmentation; TTP's SAM-LoRA; BAN's CLIP) are kept in the comparison.
With this rule the LEVIR-CD+ bar becomes \textbf{DDCDNet}~\citep{ddcdnet2024}
at $87.71$ F1 (rather than ChangeStar's $90.5$ or ChangeStar+Changen's
$91.5$).

saturated CDD benchmark we are within $0.86$ F1 of SChanger (which trains

\paragraph{LEVIR-CD+ and S2Looking — the remaining gaps.}
The two hardest benchmarks have very different gap profiles:
\textbf{LEVIR-CD+} ($-4.51$ F1 vs DDCDNet) is fundamentally a small-data
problem (637 training pairs) where the published architectures using only
the released training set cluster in the $87$--$88$ F1 range; methods that
use additional synthetic CD pretraining
(ChangeStar+Changen at $91.5$) sit roughly $+3$ F1 above this band but are
not directly comparable. \textbf{S2Looking} ($-3.24$ vs SChanger; was
$-10.17$ with our default backbone) was closed substantially by switching
to DINOv3-large, suggesting the residual gap is amenable to further
encoder improvements rather than architectural ones.

GALASH \textbf{matches or beats SOTA on DSIFN-CD ($+0.09$ F1)} and
\textbf{SECOND ($+0.08$ F1)} with only 16.5 M trainable parameters and a
frozen backbone, despite each respective SOTA (DDPM-CD, UniChange) using a
much heavier specialist pretraining or a multi-modal LLM. On the highly
saturated CDD benchmark we are within $0.87$ F1 of SChanger (which trains
for 500 epochs). On LEVIR-CD we close to within $2.2$ F1 of SChanger using
a single backbone variant; the encoder sweep below shows that
\texttt{dinov2\_large\_reg} narrows the gap further.

affects change-detection performance. Table~\ref{tab:enc-sweep} reports

\paragraph{Key findings.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Remote-sensing pretraining beats more parameters.}
    DINOv2-RS-base (86 M, RS-pretrained) achieves $90.00$ F1, beating
    DINOv2-large (304 M, generic) at $85.29$ F1. The 4$\times$ smaller
    encoder wins by $4.7$ pp.
  \item \textbf{Register tokens help generic DINOv2.} DINOv2-large with
    registers reaches $90.57$ F1, matching DINOv2-RS-base; without
    registers it sits at $85.29$. Registers contribute $5.3$ pp at the
    same parameter count.
  \item \textbf{DINOv3 has not surpassed DINOv2 on LEVIR-CD.} DINOv3-base
    is $0.15$ pp behind DINOv2-RS-base; DINOv3-large is $0.32$ pp behind
    DINOv2-large+reg. The 50\% larger pretraining corpus (lvd1689m vs
    lvd142m) does not translate to LEVIR-CD specifically.
  \item \textbf{DINOv3-sat (aerial pretraining) underperforms on
    LEVIR-CD.} It scores $88.86$ F1 --- $1.4$ pp below DINOv2-RS-base ---
    suggesting the SAT-493M corpus over-fits to coarse satellite imagery
    and loses the building-edge sensitivity LEVIR-CD requires. We show in
    \S\ref{sec:s2looking-detail} that DINOv3-sat \emph{wins} on
    S2Looking (rural, off-nadir).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The smallest decoder is the best.} Across a $5.8\times$ range

(SAM2.1-tiny) achieves the highest F1. Differences are within $0.33$ pp,
inverted with respect to capacity. The implication is unambiguous:
\textbf{SAM decoder capacity is essentially irrelevant for change
detection}; the bridge does the heavy lifting. This matches our design
hypothesis that the dense-prompt channel of SAM is a high-leverage
adaptation point.

\subsection{S2Looking and the role of aerial pretraining}
\label{sec:s2looking-detail}

S2Looking is the hardest of our six benchmarks (off-nadir + rural; $\sim$25 F1
points behind LEVIR-CD across all methods). Table~\ref{tab:s2looking}
shows that here, \emph{aerial pretraining is the differentiator}: the
DINOv3-sat encoder beats DINOv2-RS-base by $1.0$ pp on the test split, and
the gap relative to the published SOTA (UniChange, $69.32$) closes from
$10.2$ to $9.2$ pp.

This is the only benchmark where the satellite-pretrained variant wins.
We attribute this to the off-nadir + rural distribution of S2Looking
matching the SAT-493M pretraining corpus, where features such as ploughed
fields and rural roads are dominant.

multi-resolution test ($\{256, 384, 512\}$, picked by val); (3) EMA
weights from training (when available).
